## The setup

Boeing and Airbus build almost every commercial aircraft with more than 100 seats. Airlines ordering today are booking delivery slots years out. A single wide-body order can be worth billions.

A textbook would say two firms selling similar products should undercut each other until margins vanish. In practice, both report positive margins on commercial aircraft in normal years. Something is holding the price war back.

## The repeated game

The textbook result assumes a one-shot game. Aircraft is the opposite: the same two players meet in every campaign, for every airline, for decades. If Boeing undercuts Airbus on one order, Airbus can retaliate on the next fifty.

In a repeated game, cooperation (here, pricing discipline) can be stable without any agreement, because defection is punished by future retaliation. Neither firm needs to talk to the other. They just need to understand that the game never ends.

## Where the competition actually goes

Restraint on price pushes competition elsewhere. The two levers that matter are delivery slots and reliability. An airline that needs aircraft in 2028 will buy from whoever can deliver in 2028. A manufacturer that falls behind on production, or whose aircraft are grounded by regulators, loses orders without the other side cutting a single price.

That is why production problems at one manufacturer show up as a backlog surge at the other. Market share moves on execution, not on discounts.

## The suppliers in the middle

Both manufacturers buy engines from a very small number of suppliers. Those suppliers sell engines at thin margins and then earn for decades on maintenance and spare parts. In a duopoly with disciplined pricing, the question for investors is who captures the economics. The answer is often the layer below the airframe.

## What breaks the equilibrium

A credible third manufacturer changes everything. A third player makes retaliation harder to target and gives airlines a bargaining alternative. The signal to watch is not deliveries to domestic carriers, which can be mandated. It is the first large order from an airline that had a free choice.
