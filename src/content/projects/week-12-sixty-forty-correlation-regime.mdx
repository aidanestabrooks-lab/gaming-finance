## The setup

Five asset classes, each proxied by a liquid ETF: US equity (SPY), international developed equity (EFA), 7–10 year Treasuries (IEF), investment-grade credit (LQD) and gold (GLD). Their volatilities and the full correlation matrix are estimated from {st.n.toLocaleString()} daily returns between {shortDate(st.start)} and {shortDate(st.end)}, and the site re-estimates them on every build. Equities correlate strongly with each other ({st.corr[0][1].toFixed(2)} between SPY and EFA); Treasuries and investment-grade credit correlate strongly with each other ({st.corr[2][3].toFixed(2)}); gold is weakly related to everything. The one correlation that defines the regime is the stock-bond pair, and it is on a slider that starts at its latest one-year reading of {rhoNow.toFixed(2)}.

Expected returns are the input nobody observes, so the model states its method. The default is yield-based: the 10-year Treasury yield of {y10.toFixed(2)}% as the bond return, that yield plus a 4.5% premium for US equity, plus the investment-grade index spread for credit, and the 10-year breakeven inflation rate for gold. A toggle swaps in the trailing five-year compound returns instead, which for this window were {pct(st.annRet[0])} for US equity, {pct(st.annRet[2])} for Treasuries and {pct(st.annRet[4])} for gold. The two methods disagree by design; that disagreement is the point of the last section.

The portfolio problem is the Level III one: for every target return, find the weights that minimize variance.

$$
\min_w\; w^\top \Sigma w \quad \text{s.t.}\quad w^\top \mu = \mu_p,\;\; w^\top \mathbf{1} = 1
$$

Without a long-only constraint this has a closed form. Define $A = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$, $B = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, $C = \mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, $D = AC - B^2$. Then the frontier is

$$
\sigma_p^2(\mu_p) = \frac{A\mu_p^2 - 2B\mu_p + C}{D}
$$

and the tangency portfolio with a risk-free rate $r_f$ is $w_T \propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f\mathbf{1})$. The model draws that curve, the capital market line through the tangency point, and a cloud of random long-only portfolios so you can see where the constraint binds.

## Reading it

The rolling-correlation chart at the bottom of the panel is the history of the regime. Before 2021 the one-year SPY–IEF correlation averaged {rhoPre.toFixed(2)}: bonds rallied when stocks fell, and the 60/40 point sat close to the frontier with Treasuries carrying a large tangency weight. In the 2022 inflation shock it peaked at {rho2022.toFixed(2)}. Today it reads {rhoNow.toFixed(2)}.

Drag the slider to the pre-2021 value and the risk-contribution column shows the equity sleeves contributing roughly 90% of portfolio variance even though they hold 60% of capital; bonds contribute almost nothing because their covariance with equities is negative and cancels part of their own variance. Drag it past the 2022 peak toward +0.40, a fully inflation-driven regime, and three things happen. The frontier shifts right: every level of return now costs more risk. The tangency portfolio sheds Treasuries and leans into whatever is now least correlated with the rest, which in the estimated matrix is gold. And the risk contribution of the equity sleeve goes to roughly 100%; bonds are no longer a hedge, they are a smaller, lower-return version of the same bet.

## Risk budgeting

The risk contribution of asset $i$ is

$$
\text{RC}_i = \frac{w_i\,(\Sigma w)_i}{w^\top \Sigma w}
$$

and the contributions sum to one. This is the lens Level III uses for risk parity and for liability-aware allocation. The table recomputes it on every change. The question an investor should ask is not "what fraction of my capital is in bonds" but "what fraction of my risk is in equities", and in the positive-correlation regime the honest answer is: almost all of it.

## The sensitivity you cannot avoid

Mean-variance optimizers are notoriously driven by expected returns, which are the inputs nobody knows. Flip the method toggle and watch the tangency weights swing; the five-year trailing returns reflect a window in which Treasuries lost money and gold compounded at {pct(st.annRet[4])}, and an optimizer fed those numbers will tell you to own very little duration. Nudge the equity expected return by 50 basis points and the weights move again. That instability is not a reason to abandon the framework. It is a reason to use it for the thing it is good at, which is showing how the *structure* of risk changes with correlation, and to treat the weights it spits out as a direction rather than a destination.

## What this means for investors

Decide which regime you believe you are in. If inflation risk dominates, bonds are duration exposure rather than a hedge, and the diversifiers that matter are the ones uncorrelated with both growth and inflation shocks. If growth risk dominates, the old regime returns and Treasuries resume their role. The rolling chart tells you where the market has been; the slider is your belief about where it goes; the frontier is its consequence.
