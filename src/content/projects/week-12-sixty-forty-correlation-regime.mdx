## The setup

Five asset classes with illustrative expected returns, volatilities, and a correlation matrix. Equities correlate strongly with each other; Treasuries and investment-grade credit correlate strongly with each other; gold is weakly related to everything. The one correlation that defines the regime is the stock-bond pair, and it is on a slider.

The portfolio problem is the Level III one: for every target return, find the weights that minimize variance.

$$
\min_w\; w^\top \Sigma w \quad \text{s.t.}\quad w^\top \mu = \mu_p,\;\; w^\top \mathbf{1} = 1
$$

Without a long-only constraint this has a closed form. Define $A = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$, $B = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, $C = \mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, $D = AC - B^2$. Then the frontier is

$$
\sigma_p^2(\mu_p) = \frac{A\mu_p^2 - 2B\mu_p + C}{D}
$$

and the tangency portfolio with a risk-free rate $r_f$ is $w_T \propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f\mathbf{1})$. The model draws that curve, the capital market line through the tangency point, and a cloud of random long-only portfolios so you can see where the constraint binds.

## Reading it

Start at the default correlation of −0.20, the post-2000 regime. The 60/40 point sits close to the frontier and Treasuries carry a large tangency weight. The risk-contribution column shows the equity sleeves contributing roughly 90% of portfolio variance even though they hold 60% of capital; bonds contribute almost nothing because their covariance with equities is negative and cancels part of their own variance.

Now drag the correlation to +0.40, the regime of 2022 and of any inflation-driven sell-off. Three things happen. The frontier shifts right: every level of return now costs more risk. The tangency portfolio sheds Treasuries and leans into gold, because gold is now the asset least correlated with the rest. And the risk contribution of the equity sleeve goes to roughly 100%; bonds are no longer a hedge, they are a smaller, lower-return version of the same bet.

## Risk budgeting

The risk contribution of asset $i$ is

$$
\text{RC}_i = \frac{w_i\,(\Sigma w)_i}{w^\top \Sigma w}
$$

and the contributions sum to one. This is the lens Level III uses for risk parity and for liability-aware allocation. The table recomputes it on every change. The question an investor should ask is not "what fraction of my capital is in bonds" but "what fraction of my risk is in equities", and in the positive-correlation regime the honest answer is: almost all of it.

## The sensitivity you cannot avoid

Mean-variance optimizers are notoriously driven by expected returns, which are the inputs nobody knows. Nudge the equity expected return by 50 basis points and watch the tangency weights swing. That instability is not a reason to abandon the framework. It is a reason to use it for the thing it is good at, which is showing how the *structure* of risk changes with correlation, and to treat the weights it spits out as a direction rather than a destination.

## What this means for investors

Decide which regime you believe you are in. If inflation risk dominates, bonds are duration exposure rather than a hedge, and the diversifiers that matter are the ones uncorrelated with both growth and inflation shocks. If growth risk dominates, the old regime returns and Treasuries resume their role. The slider is the belief; the frontier is its consequence.
