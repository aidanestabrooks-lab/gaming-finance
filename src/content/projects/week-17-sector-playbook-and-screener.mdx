export const ols = (X, y) => {
  const k = X[0].length + 1;
  const rows = X.map((r) => [1, ...r]);
  const A = Array.from({ length: k }, () => Array(k).fill(0));
  const b = Array(k).fill(0);
  rows.forEach((r, i) => { for (let a = 0; a < k; a++) { b[a] += r[a] * y[i]; for (let c = 0; c < k; c++) A[a][c] += r[a] * r[c]; } });
  const beta = solve(A, b);
  const fit = rows.map((r) => r.reduce((s, v, j) => s + v * beta[j], 0));
  const sse = y.reduce((s, v, i) => s + (v - fit[i]) ** 2, 0);
  const my = mean(y);
  const sst = y.reduce((s, v) => s + (v - my) ** 2, 0);
  const s2 = sse / (y.length - k);
  const se = beta.map((_, j) => { const e = Array(k).fill(0); e[j] = 1; return Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, s2 * solve(A.map((r) => [...r]), e)[j])); });
  return { beta, t: beta.map((v, j) => (se[j] ? v / se[j] : 0)), r2: sst ? 1 - sse / sst : 0, sigma: Math.sqrt(s2) };
};

## The setup

Sector allocation is the part of portfolio construction where macro views are supposed to turn into positions, and it is usually done by narrative: rates up, buy banks; oil up, buy energy; recession coming, buy staples and utilities. Each of those is a testable claim about a regression coefficient. This week estimates the coefficients, shows their standard errors, and then does the thing narratives never do, which is check how the model would have done out of sample.

The universe is the eleven SPDR sector ETFs, which together are the S&P 500. For each sector the dependent variable is its monthly return in excess of SPY, so the model explains relative performance, not the market's direction. The five explanatory variables are the month's change in the 10-year Treasury yield, the change in the Baa corporate spread over Treasuries, the log change of the broad trade-weighted dollar index, the log change of WTI crude, and the change in the VIX. The sample runs from {p.months[0]} to {p.months[n - 1]}, {n} months, which is as far back as the Communication Services ETF exists. All of it is re-estimated in your browser from a panel the site rebuilds every day.

## Why sectors load differently on macro factors

The betas are not arbitrary. Each has an economic mechanism, and knowing the mechanism is what lets you decide whether a coefficient that the data cannot pin down is worth believing anyway.

**Rates.** A sector's sensitivity to the 10-year yield comes from two places: the duration of its cash flows and the duration of its liabilities. Utilities and real estate have long, stable, regulated or contracted cash flows and carry a lot of debt, so they trade like long bonds; when yields rise, their discount rate rises and their refinancing cost rises at the same time. Technology has long-duration cash flows too, but of a different kind, far in the future and growing, so its rate beta depends on whether the move in yields reflects growth, which helps, or inflation, which does not. Financials run the other way: banks earn a spread between what they pay on deposits and what they earn on loans, and a steeper or higher curve widens that spread, so they tend to have a positive rate beta even though a hiking cycle eventually hurts their borrowers.

**Credit.** The Baa spread over Treasuries is the market's price of default risk. Sectors full of levered, cyclical balance sheets, consumer discretionary, industrials, materials and real estate, are hurt when that price rises, because their own borrowing costs rise and because a widening spread is usually a recession signal. Consumer staples, health care and utilities sell things people buy in a recession, so their relative return tends to be positive exactly when the spread widens. The credit beta is in effect a recession beta.

**The dollar.** Roughly 40% of S&P 500 revenue comes from outside the United States, but the share differs sharply by sector. Technology and materials earn a large fraction abroad and are hurt when the dollar strengthens, both in translation and in competitiveness. Utilities, real estate and financials are nearly all domestic. Energy and materials carry an extra channel: commodities are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar usually means a weaker commodity price, which compounds the translation effect.

**Oil.** The energy sector's earnings are a direct function of the oil price, so its beta to WTI is the largest and most reliable coefficient in the table. The other side of the ledger is fuel-intensive industries: airlines inside industrials, chemicals inside materials, and consumer discretionary when gasoline eats into household budgets.

**Volatility.** The VIX is not a fundamental, it is a measure of fear, but it captures the defensive rotation that none of the other factors fully explain. When volatility spikes, investors sell what they own the most of and what has the highest beta, and they buy the sectors that behave like bonds. A negative VIX beta is a cyclical sector; a positive one is a defensive sector, whatever its balance sheet says.

## A worked scenario: the hike-pricing regime

## Why a scenario forecast is conditional

Three things about that equation matter more than the ranking it produces.

It conditions on the shock. If the 10-year rises 50 basis points next month, the model says which sectors should outperform; it says nothing about whether the 10-year will rise. The factor forecast is your job. Today the Baa spread is {lv.BAA10Y.toFixed(2)} percentage points, the broad dollar index {lv.DTWEXBGS.toFixed(1)}, WTI ${lv.DCOILWTICO.toFixed(0)} and the VIX {lv.VIXCLS.toFixed(1)}; the presets are round-number shocks from those levels, not predictions.

It carries the residual. Even with the factors known, the band around each bar is one residual standard deviation, and most bars overlap. The out-of-sample check under the chart refits the model each month from {p.months[36]} on and ranks the sectors using that month's realized factor changes. If the betas were unstable the hit rate would sit at 50% and the rank correlation at zero; the numbers reported there are the right summary of how much the betas are worth, and they are smaller than the in-sample fit.

It is linear. A 100 basis point move is treated as twice a 50 basis point move, and a rise in yields driven by growth is treated the same as one driven by inflation. Level II's own caveat applies: regressions describe the sample's range, and this sample contains a hiking cycle, a pandemic, an inflation shock and an AI capex boom, but not everything, and not in the combinations that will arrive next.

## Momentum, honestly

The second model asks a different question: ignoring macro, does a sector's own recent performance predict its next month? Jegadeesh and Titman found in 1993 that US stocks with the best prior-year returns, skipping the latest month, kept outperforming for the next three to twelve months, and that the latest month tended to reverse. Moskowitz and Grinblatt showed in 1999 that much of that effect was industry momentum: buying the winning industries and selling the losing ones captured most of the profit. Sector ETFs are the cheapest way to express that finding.

Pooled across all eleven sectors and every month since the window could be estimated, the momentum coefficient is {mom.beta[1].toFixed(3)} (t = {mom.t[1].toFixed(1)}) and the one-month reversal coefficient is {mom.beta[2].toFixed(3)} (t = {mom.t[2].toFixed(1)}), on {mom.n.toLocaleString()} sector-months. A positive momentum coefficient agrees with the literature in sign; a positive reversal coefficient would disagree with it. Whether either is {tf(mom.t[1])} at conventional levels is reported next to it.

Those are the full-sample numbers. The ones that matter are out of sample: the rank correlation between what the expanding-window model predicted and what happened the next month, and the backtest of holding the top three sectors against equal weight. Both are in the panel, and both deserve the caveat printed under them. Eleven assets and roughly a hundred months is a small panel in two ways. Cross-sectionally, a rank correlation on eleven items is coarse: moving one sector by one place changes it by a visible amount. Through time, a hundred months contains perhaps three or four distinct regimes, so the backtest's result is dominated by whichever sector led for longest. The published momentum evidence uses thousands of stocks and decades of data. A rank correlation of 0.05 here is consistent with that evidence and also consistent with nothing; it is not a trading rule.

The blended ranking at the bottom of the panel standardizes the two forecasts and mixes them with a weight you set. At 100% scenario it is the macro model; at 0% it is momentum. In between it is a transparent version of what most sector-rotation products sell, with the difference that you can see both ingredients and both sets of diagnostics.

Five pillars, each a z-score averaged over its metrics: value (P/E, EV/EBITDA, free-cash-flow yield), growth (revenue growth), quality (EBIT margin, return on equity, interest coverage, leverage), momentum (12-1 return and position in the 52-week range) and low risk (beta and volatility). Filters apply before scoring, so a screen for leverage below two times EBITDA and positive free-cash-flow yield scores only the survivors.

Two screens worth running. The first is quality at a reasonable price: EBIT margin above 15%, return on equity above 15%, net debt below two times EBITDA, and a P/E below 25. On today's data {qarp.length} of the {nStocks} names pass, and sorted by P/E the cheapest are {list(qarp)}{qarp.length > 6 ? ', and others' : ''}. Set the quality weight to 2 and value to 1.5, zero the momentum pillar, and the score orders them by how much quality you get per unit of multiple. The second is a low-volatility income screen: beta below 0.8, volatility below 28%, free-cash-flow yield above 3.5%. {lowvol.length} names pass, led by free-cash-flow yield: {list(lowvol)}. Weight low risk at 2 and value at 1, and the result is a list of businesses that fund their own payouts and do not move with the index, which is the raw material for the income sleeve of a portfolio.

Read what the screens return against the sector model. If the quality screen is dominated by energy names, it is telling you that oil-sensitive cash flows are currently cheap relative to their quality, and the energy row of the heatmap tells you what you would be exposed to. If the low-volatility screen is full of staples and utilities, you are buying the sectors with the highest credit and VIX betas, which protect in a downturn and lag in a melt-up.

A screener does one thing: it ranks on what is measurable today. It does not know that a cheap name is cheap because a lawsuit is pending, that a high-momentum name just closed an acquisition that changes every trailing number, or that a high free-cash-flow yield is a one-time working-capital release. Treat the top decile as the list that deserves the Week 6 reverse DCF and the Week 10 credit lens, not as a portfolio.

## Limitations

Everything above is estimated on monthly data with ordinary least squares, which assumes the errors are uncorrelated and have constant variance. Financial returns violate both, mildly, so the t-statistics are somewhat too generous. The factors are contemporaneous: the model relates this month's sector return to this month's factor changes, which makes it a tool for scenario analysis, not a forecast of returns from today's information. The sample is short because of the Communication Services ETF's 2018 listing, and it includes a reclassification of several large technology names into that sector, which changed what the ETFs hold. The screener's fundamentals are trailing and as filed, so a company that restated or changed its fiscal year can carry a stale number for a quarter, and a fixed universe of {nStocks} names chosen for sector coverage is not an index and has survivorship built in. None of this makes the models wrong. It makes them what they are: a structured way to state and test a view, with the uncertainty attached.

## What this means for investors

Most sector narratives are claims about betas, and the betas can be estimated. Some are strong enough to act on, many are not, and the regression tells you which is which. When you hold a macro view, run it through the scenario tool and look at the band, not just the ranking: if the band of your favored sector overlaps the band of the one you would sell, you do not have a sector trade, you have a factor bet with a sector label, and you should size it like one. The hike-pricing scenario currently favors {hike[0].name} over {hike[10].name} by about {fmtB(hike[0].v - hike[10].v, 1)} a month, with roughly a {((hike[0].v - hike[10].v) / Math.sqrt(hike[0].s ** 2 + hike[10].s ** 2)).toFixed(1)}-sigma margin; that is the kind of number to carry into position sizing. Momentum in sectors is real in the literature and faint in this sample; if you use it, use it as a tiebreaker. And when the screener hands you a short list, remember that it got there by arithmetic on last year's numbers. The work that creates value starts where the screener stops.
