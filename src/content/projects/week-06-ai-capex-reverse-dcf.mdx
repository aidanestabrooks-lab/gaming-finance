## The setup

The AI build-out is the largest private capital-spending program in the history of the technology sector. The firms doing the spending are valued as if that capital will earn a return well above its cost. Whether it does is the entire question, and a free-cash-flow model is the honest way to ask it.

The Level II curriculum defines free cash flow to the firm as

$$
\text{FCFF}_t = \text{EBIT}_t(1 - \tau) + \text{Dep}_t - \text{Capex}_t - \Delta\text{NWC}_t
$$

The model below rolls depreciation into the margin and collapses the last two terms into a single reinvestment line driven by a **sales-to-capital ratio**: every incremental dollar of revenue requires $1 / \text{(sales-to-capital)}$ dollars of new capital. A hyperscaler building data centers has a low ratio; a software firm has a high one. That ratio is the capex-intensity dial, and it is the one that matters.

## How the pieces fit

Stage 1 runs five years at the growth rate you set. Stage 2 fades linearly to the terminal rate over the number of years you choose. Each year's reinvestment is $\Delta\text{Revenue} / \text{S2C}$. The terminal value is a Gordon growth value on the first post-fade cash flow, with the reinvestment rate set to the level consistent with steady-state growth:

$$
\text{Reinvestment rate}_\infty = \frac{g}{\text{ROIC}}, \qquad \text{ROIC} = \text{margin} \times (1-\tau) \times \text{S2C}
$$

That last identity is the hinge. If ROIC falls below WACC, every dollar of growth reduces value. Set the sales-to-capital ratio to 0.6 with a 30% margin and a 21% tax rate: ROIC is 14.2%, still above a 9% WACC, and growth helps. Drop the ratio to 0.35: ROIC is 8.3%, below WACC, and you will watch the per-share value fall as you *raise* the growth rate. That is the arithmetic of value-destroying growth, and it is the risk the market is pricing when it reacts to a capex guidance raise.

## The sensitivity grid

The table shows value per share across WACC and terminal growth. The spread between the top-left and bottom-right cells is typically several multiples of the central value. That is not a flaw in the model. It is the correct statement of how uncertain a long-duration asset's value is, and why these stocks move so much on a 25-basis-point change in the discount rate.

## Reverse DCF

Instead of guessing growth and computing a value, fix the value at the market price and solve for growth. The model does this by bisection on the stage-1 growth rate. The output is the growth the market is already paying for. If that number is higher than any historical five-year run for a company this size, you have learned something: the price embeds a forecast nobody has achieved. If it is lower than the company's current growth, the market may be pricing a capex-driven collapse in ROIC rather than a slowdown in revenue.

## What this means for investors

Hold the model's two most sensitive inputs in your head: sales-to-capital and the fade. Every AI capex headline is a change to the first. Every "margin expansion story" is a claim about the second. Translating headlines into those two numbers, and then into value per share, is what separates a valuation from a vibe.
