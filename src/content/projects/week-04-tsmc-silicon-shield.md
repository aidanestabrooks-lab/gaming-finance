## The setup

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company produces the large majority of the world's most advanced logic chips. Its customers include nearly every major technology company. Almost all of that leading-edge capacity sits in Taiwan.

The phrase "silicon shield" describes the theory that this dependence protects Taiwan: no major power can afford a conflict that would destroy the fabs. Whether that theory holds is a question for strategists. What it does to the firm's economics is a question for investors.

## The firm as a strategic actor

Most companies are players in a market. TSMC is also a piece on a geopolitical board. Governments in the United States, Japan, and Europe have offered it billions in subsidies to build capacity on their soil. That is not industrial policy as usual. It is an attempt to buy insurance against a single point of failure.

For TSMC, this is a mixed blessing. Subsidized fabs expand its footprint at someone else's expense. But overseas fabs cost more to run, take longer to ramp, and lag the Taiwan fabs technologically. The firm has acknowledged that margins outside Taiwan are lower.

## The deterrence game

Deterrence works when every player believes disruption is worse than the status quo. Today that belief is strong: the global economy would absorb a shock no one wants to test. That makes "no disruption" the equilibrium.

But equilibria depend on payoffs, and payoffs change. Every fab built outside Taiwan, and every step a competitor takes toward the leading edge, lowers the cost of disruption for the rest of the world. The shield gets thinner as the insurance gets better. That is the irony at the center of the story.

## How to think about exposure

Investors often treat this as a TSMC question. It is a portfolio question. The chip designers, the device makers, the cloud providers, and the software companies that depend on them all carry the same concentration risk indirectly. A diversified technology portfolio is more exposed to one island than its holdings list suggests.

## The signpost

Competitor capacity at the leading edge is the variable to track. Not announcements, which are cheap, but yield rates and volume shipments, which are not. Those move in years. Until they move, the equilibrium holds.
