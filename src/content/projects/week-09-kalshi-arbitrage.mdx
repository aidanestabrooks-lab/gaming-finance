## The setup

A Kalshi contract pays $1 if an event happens and $0 otherwise, so its price is the market's probability. Markets with several outcomes (the five possible results of one FOMC meeting: cut 50 or more, cut 25, hold, hike 25, hike 50 or more) are a partition: exactly one resolves YES. The prices of a partition should sum to $1. They often do not, because each outcome has its own order book, its own liquidity, and its own crowd.

The tools below are loaded with the real quotes. As of {shortDate(kalshi.fetched)}, the YES asks on "{ev.title}" sum to {sumAsk}¢ and the YES bids to {sumBid}¢; the partition is priced correctly when $1.00 sits between those two numbers after fees. The snapshot is refreshed with each daily rebuild, not streamed, so treat it as the shape of the market rather than an executable quote.

The fee is where most of the free money goes. Kalshi charges, per order,

$$
\text{Fee} = \left\lceil\, 0.07 \times C \times P \times (1 - P) \,\right\rceil_{\text{cents}}
$$

where $C$ is contracts and $P$ is the price in dollars. The product $P(1-P)$ peaks at $0.25$ when $P = 0.5$, so a 50¢ contract costs 1.75¢ in fees and a 95¢ contract costs about 0.33¢. Fees punish mid-priced buckets and spare the tails.

## Tool 1: the bucket scanner

Enter the YES ask for each bucket. The scanner computes two trades. Buy every YES: cost is the sum of prices plus fees, payout is exactly $1 per contract set. Buy every NO: cost is the sum of $(1 - P_i)$ plus fees, and payout is $(N-1)$ dollars because every bucket but one resolves NO.

<BucketArb id="fed-buckets" />

The YES column holds each outcome's actual ask and the NO column its actual NO ask, which is not simply one dollar minus the YES ask: the two sides have their own spreads. Switch between the upcoming meetings in the dropdown. Near-dated meetings tend to have one outcome priced near 90¢ and the rest near 1¢, where fees are tiny; further-out meetings spread the probability across outcomes, which is exactly where the quadratic fee bites hardest. The band of fee-free sums is reported at the bottom right: any sum inside it is noise, not opportunity. Edit a cell to test a quote you see live.

## Tool 2: cross-venue arbitrage

The same event trades on two venues at different prices. Buy YES where it is cheaper and NO where it is dearer; the payout is $1 regardless of the outcome.

<CrossVenueArb id="cross" />

Both venues quote the same five outcomes of the next FOMC decision, and the table shows every outcome's bid and ask side by side. The calculator evaluates both directions, YES on Kalshi with NO on Polymarket and the reverse, each leg at that venue's real ask, and keeps the cheaper one. On the most-traded outcome the Kalshi YES ask is {kAsk}¢ against a Polymarket ask of {pAsk}¢. The break-even condition is that the two legs plus fees cost less than the $1 payout. Below it, a trade that is "right" on both legs still loses. Above it, the return is riskless but the capital is locked until the market closes, and the days-to-resolution slider is preset to that date. The annualized figure is the one to compare against Treasury bills, and it is frequently lower.

## Tool 3: Kelly sizing when the edge is not riskless

Most of the time you do not have an arbitrage. You have a belief that differs from the price. If you think an event has probability $p$ and the market prices it at $m$, the growth-optimal fraction of bankroll to put on YES is

$$
f^* = \frac{p - m}{1 - m}
$$

and on NO, when $p < m$, it is $f^* = (m - p)/m$. Expected log growth per bet is $g(f) = p\ln(1 + fb) + (1-p)\ln(1 - f)$ with $b = (1-m)/m$. The chart shows the whole curve: full Kelly sits at the peak, half Kelly earns about three quarters of the growth with far less variance, and anything beyond full Kelly is strictly worse.

<KellySizer id="kelly" bankroll={10000} />

The default edge is not invented: the market price is Kalshi's YES ask on the "Hike 25 bp" outcome and the belief is Polymarket's mid on the same outcome. Two venues, one event, two probabilities. If you trust the deeper book you have a Kelly stake on the other one; if the gap is a cent or two, full Kelly is still a small fraction of bankroll, which is the point.

## The game theory underneath

Why do these gaps exist at all? Because each bucket's order book is a separate game with separate players. A trader with a strong view on one range pushes that bucket without touching the others. Arbitrageurs are the players who enforce the partition constraint, and their incentive to do so is exactly the after-fee profit above. When fees exceed the gap, nobody enforces it, and the partition can stay mispriced for days. That is why the inefficiencies cluster in mid-priced buckets of thin markets.

## What this means for investors

Prediction markets are a probability feed with a fee schedule bolted on. The riskless trades are rare, small, and capital-hungry once annualized; on the Fed partition above they usually fail the fee test outright. The real use for most investors is the third tool: a disciplined way to size a view against a market price, which is the same problem as sizing any trade, with the advantage that the payoff is binary and the math is exact.
