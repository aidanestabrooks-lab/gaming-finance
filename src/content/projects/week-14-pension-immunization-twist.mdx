## The setup

A defined-benefit plan must pay a fixed annual benefit starting in a few years and continuing for a couple of decades. The sponsor wants to hold a bond portfolio such that, whatever rates do, assets stay ahead of liabilities. The sponsor has two candidate portfolios of the same present value and the same duration: a **bullet**, one bond maturing near the liability's duration, and a **barbell**, a short bond and a long bond mixed to the same duration.

## The immunization conditions

With a flat yield $y$, the liability stream $L_t$ has present value, Macaulay duration, and convexity

$$
PV_L = \sum_t \frac{L_t}{(1+y)^t}, \qquad
D_L = \frac{1}{PV_L}\sum_t \frac{t\,L_t}{(1+y)^t}, \qquad
C_L = \frac{1}{PV_L(1+y)^2}\sum_t \frac{t(t+1)\,L_t}{(1+y)^t}
$$

Classical immunization sets asset $PV$ and $D$ equal to the liability's and asset convexity above it. The barbell weights are solved from two equations (value match, duration match) in two unknowns. The bullet is the single par bond whose duration is nearest the liability's. Both satisfy the first two conditions. The "convexity gap" box shows that only the barbell satisfies the third with room to spare.

## Parallel shifts

Drag the parallel-shift slider with the twist at zero. The right-hand chart shows surplus against the shift. Both portfolios stay near zero surplus for small shifts, which is immunization doing its job. For large shifts, the barbell's surplus curves upward on both sides: its higher convexity means it gains more when yields fall and loses less when they rise. This is the textbook argument for convexity, and if parallel shifts were the only risk, the barbell would be the clear choice.

## Twists

Now set the parallel shift to zero and drag the twist. The twist steepens or flattens the curve around the liability's duration: short yields move one way, long yields the other. The left-hand chart shows surplus against the twist. The bullet barely moves, because its single cash flow sits at the pivot. The barbell swings hard, because half its value is at the short end and half at the long end, exactly where the twist bites. A steepener of 50 basis points per decade of maturity can wipe out a surplus the barbell had earned from years of carry.

This is **structural risk**: the exposure of a duration-matched portfolio to changes in the shape of the curve. The Level III reading's remedy is to minimize the dispersion of asset cash flows around the liability's, which, taken to the limit, is cash-flow matching. Convexity and dispersion are two sides of one coin; you cannot buy the first without the second.

## What this means for investors

If you sponsor, advise, or simply hold the equity of a company with a large pension, ask how its LDI program is built. A duration-matched portfolio of long Treasuries and short credit is a barbell. It looks immunized on the actuary's report and it is immunized against the one shock the report measures. Against the shock that typically accompanies a policy pivot, the twist, it is not. For an investor in long-duration bonds generally, the lesson is the same in miniature: duration tells you about level; it is silent about shape.
