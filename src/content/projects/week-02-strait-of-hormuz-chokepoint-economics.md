## The setup

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the open ocean. Nearly every barrel exported by sea from Saudi Arabia's east coast, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, the UAE's Gulf ports and Iran passes through it. The EIA puts total oil flows at 20.7 million barrels a day in 2024 and 20.9 million in the first half of 2025, about 20 percent of global petroleum liquids consumption and a quarter of all seaborne traded oil. Add 11.4 billion cubic feet a day of liquefied natural gas, more than 20 percent of global LNG trade, primarily from Qatar.

The alternatives are small. The Saudi East-West pipeline to the Red Sea and the UAE's pipeline to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman together provide about 4.7 million b/d of capacity to bypass the strait. Iran's Goreh-Jask line has an effective capacity of around 0.3 million b/d. The UAE plans another 1.5 million b/d line to Fujairah by 2027. Even with everything running full, roughly three quarters of the current flow has no other way out.

Every few years someone threatens to close it. The strait has never been closed for long. That is not luck. It is the equilibrium of a specific game, and the game has a name.

## The hold-up problem

Economists call it a hold-up. One party controls an asset that another party cannot operate without, and the relationship cannot be exited quickly. The controlling party can demand a share of the surplus simply by threatening to withhold. The textbook example is a single supplier of a custom part to a factory built around that part. Hormuz is the same structure with tankers instead of parts.

The key result of hold-up theory is that the controlling party's bargaining power is bounded by the other side's outside option. If the factory can retool for a different part in a month at a cost of X, the supplier can never extract more than X. The threat is only as strong as the alternatives are weak.

That is what makes Hormuz different from every other chokepoint on the chart. The Red Sea route has an outside option: sail around Africa. The Panama Canal has one: Suez or the Cape. Malacca has the Sunda and Lombok straits. Hormuz has 4.7 million b/d of pipeline, a 15-day detour that does not exist because there is no sea route, and nothing else. The outside option is to not export the oil, which is why the threat carries so much weight.

But the hold-up has a second side. The party making the threat also cannot exit the relationship. Iran's own exports pass through the same water. So do the exports of the Gulf states that would be the first to call for a naval response. Closing the strait is not withholding a part; it is burning down the factory that the supplier also works in.

## A worked payoff

Reduce the game to one actor who can close the strait and one coalition of importers. The closer has two moves: keep threatening, or close. The importers can concede something in a negotiation, or resist.

Assign stylized annual values. Let the threat, kept alive by occasional harassment of shipping, extract concessions worth $L$ in whatever negotiation is underway, at near-zero cost. Let closure cost the closer its own export revenue $E$, a military response $M$, and a permanent loss $D$ as buyers accelerate bypass pipelines and alternative suppliers. Let closure extract a one-time concession $C$.

| Closer's payoff | Importers concede | Importers resist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Threaten** | $+L$ | $0$ |
| **Close** | $C - E - M - D$ | $-E - M - D$ |

Closing is rational only when $C > E + M + D$ and the importers concede, which requires that the stake on the table is larger than the closer's entire export economy plus the cost of a war plus the permanent erosion of the asset. For a state whose budget runs on those exports, that condition is met only when regime survival is the stake. In every other state of the world, the top row dominates. The rational strategy is to threaten forever, harass enough to keep the threat credible, and act almost never.

The importers understand this, which is why they resist more often than they concede. The result is a stable equilibrium of persistent threat and rare action. That is the pattern the strait has shown for decades.

## The option-value view

A second framing gives the same answer from finance. The ability to close the strait is a real option. Its holder can exercise at any time; exercising is costly and irreversible; the payoff from exercising depends on circumstances that change. An option like that has intrinsic value, what exercise would yield today, and time value, the value of being able to wait and choose a better moment.

For Hormuz, intrinsic value is usually negative: exercising today destroys $E + M + D$. The entire worth of the option is time value, the leverage from being able to exercise at the one moment when it would pay. Exercise kills the time value. An option is worth more alive than dead, and so is the threat.

The implication for markets is direct. What is priced into crude is not the cost of closure. It is the probability of exercise, times the severity of the disruption, times its expected duration. Write it as

$$
\text{Risk premium} \approx p \times S \times \tau
$$

where $p$ is the probability of a disruption in the relevant window, $S$ the price impact per unit time while it lasts, and $\tau$ its expected duration. Since $p$ is small almost all the time and $\tau$ is bounded by how fast the closer's own costs mount, the premium is modest, volatile, and prone to collapse the moment $p$ is marked down.

## Three episodes

**Abqaiq, September 2019.** On September 14, 2019, drone and missile strikes hit Saudi Aramco's Abqaiq processing facility and the Khurais oil field. Abqaiq has a capacity of about 7 million b/d, about 7 percent of global crude oil production capacity, and was running at roughly 4.9 million b/d. The attack took 5.7 million b/d offline, more than half of Saudi output and about 5 percent of global supply. Brent closed at \$60.22 on Friday, September 13 and \$69.02 on Monday, September 16, a rise of about 14 percent, the largest one-day gain in a decade. By September 17 Aramco reported Abqaiq producing 2 million b/d with full capacity expected by the end of the month, and tanker loadings were back to pre-attack levels. Prices fell that day. The lesson is in the $\tau$ term. A disruption with a short expected duration, even a very large one, cannot sustain a premium.

**The Red Sea, 2024.** Houthi attacks on commercial shipping began in November 2023. By December crude flows through Bab el-Mandeb were about 18 percent below the January-to-November average and clean product flows 30 percent lower. For 2024 as a whole, EIA data show oil flows through Bab el-Mandeb fell to 4.1 million b/d from 9.3 million in 2023, Suez and SUMED fell to 4.8 million from 8.8 million, and the Cape of Good Hope route rose to 9.3 million from 6.2 million. The oil did not stop moving. It went the long way, adding about 15 days to an Arabian Sea to Europe voyage. Brent was \$82 in the week ending November 17, 2023, before the attacks, and \$79 on January 18, 2024. Red Sea tanker rates rose about 20 percent in December, and war-risk premiums for the Red Sea reached around 1 percent of hull value in 2024. The cost showed up in freight and insurance, not in the crude price, because the outside option existed and was used. This is hold-up theory in a single year of data. A chokepoint with an alternative route is a toll, not a hostage.

**Hormuz, June 2025.** After U.S. strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities at Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan, Iran's parliament voted on Sunday, June 22, 2025, to approve closing the Strait of Hormuz. Brent futures rose more than 9 percent that afternoon. The final decision rested with the Supreme National Security Council. War-risk premiums for voyages into the Gulf, which had sat around 0.2 to 0.3 percent of vessel value for months, jumped to 0.5 percent within a week. The strait was not closed. The parliament's vote was the threat row of the payoff table, performed in public; the council's restraint was the recognition that the close row did not pay. The premium that formed on the Sunday had nothing to feed on once that became clear.

## Who carries the physical risk

The price of crude is global. The barrels are not. In the first half of 2025, about 89 percent of crude and condensate through Hormuz went to Asian markets, and China, India, Japan and South Korea together took 74 percent. The United States imported about 0.4 million b/d of Persian Gulf crude through the strait, roughly 7 percent of its crude imports. Almost a third of the LNG bound for China came through Hormuz.

A closure would therefore be two different events. For Asian refiners and utilities it is a supply event: the crude and LNG they run do not arrive, and the question is inventory and alternative cargoes. For U.S. and European producers it is a price event: their output is unaffected and sells for more. For U.S. refiners it is a spread event: domestic crude remains available while product prices rise globally.

This asymmetry explains why the geopolitical premium is often smaller than intuition suggests. The marginal buyers with the most to lose also hold strategic stocks, and the region's Saudi East-West pipeline has been used before: during the 2024 Red Sea disruption, Saudi crude through Bab el-Mandeb fell by more than half while its volumes through Suez and SUMED fell only about 10 percent, because exports were shifted to the pipeline and Red Sea terminals. Flexibility exists. It is simply much smaller than the flow.

## What to watch

Observable indicators, in roughly the order they move:

- **War-risk insurance premiums** for Gulf transits, quoted as a percent of hull and machinery value per voyage. The Gulf baseline has been around 0.2 to 0.3 percent; the June 2025 spike was 0.5 percent; the Red Sea in 2024 reached about 1 percent. This is the market's $p$ in real time, set by underwriters with capital at risk.
- **Daily tanker transit counts and AIS behavior** in the strait: ships loitering outside, switching off transponders, or waiting for escort.
- **The Brent-Dubai spread and the shape of the Brent curve.** Prompt backwardation that steepens without a change in inventories is a disruption premium, not a demand signal.
- **Utilization of the bypass pipelines.** Saudi East-West and UAE Fujairah flows rise when exporters are hedging route risk. New capacity, such as the planned 1.5 million b/d Fujairah line by 2027, lowers the ceiling on the hold-up permanently.
- **Strategic stock releases and Asian buyer behavior**, especially Chinese import pace and LNG spot prices in Northeast Asia.
- **The institutional channel.** A parliamentary vote is a threat. A Supreme National Security Council order, a naval mining operation or an announced exclusion zone is exercise. The distinction is the whole game.

## What this means for investors

The chokepoint is a hold-up whose leverage rests on the absence of an outside option, exercised by a party that cannot exit the relationship either. That structure produces frequent threats, rare action, and a crude price that carries a small, volatile premium which forms in hours and decays in weeks. Positioning for the premium is a bet on $p$, and $p$ is marked down the moment the threat is not carried out.

**Integrated majors** with production across several continents are hedged against a Gulf disruption in volume terms and benefit in price terms. They are the lowest-beta way to hold the exposure, and the one least likely to give back a scare rally.

**Pure-play producers** are levered to price, not to the route. U.S. shale and non-Gulf producers gain from a Hormuz premium with no physical risk. Gulf-based producers, including listed national champions, carry the opposite profile: the price rises, but their barrels are the ones that cannot leave. The East-West pipeline mitigates this for Saudi output and for almost nobody else.

**Tankers** are the highest-variance instrument. Rates spike on disruption, as the roughly 20 percent jump in Red Sea tanker rates in December 2023 showed, and fall back when routes normalize. A Red Sea-style diversion is good for tanker owners: longer voyages absorb fleet capacity. A Hormuz closure is different: it strands the ships inside and removes a quarter of seaborne cargo. Owners of vessels outside the Gulf gain; those with ships inside lose. The direction depends on which chokepoint and on the fleet's position.

**Refiners** split by geography. Asian refiners running Gulf crude face a supply problem that no price can solve quickly. U.S. refiners with access to domestic crude face widening crack spreads. European refiners sit in between, with the 2024 experience showing that longer routes raise their feedstock cost without stopping supply.

**LNG** deserves its own line. More than a fifth of global LNG trade passes through Hormuz, and Qatar has no pipeline alternative. A disruption hits Asian LNG importers and benefits U.S. and Australian LNG exporters, whose cargoes become the marginal supply.

For a diversified portfolio the practical conclusion is about timing, not direction. Energy exposure bought at the peak of a Hormuz scare is a position whose thesis depends on a government choosing the bottom row of the payoff table. Decades of behavior say it will not, and the market knows that, which is why the premium is small to begin with. The structural trade is the slow one: bypass capacity, strategic stocks, and the gradual shift of Asian buyers toward Atlantic Basin supply all shrink the hold-up over years. Watch those the way you would watch a competitor adding factories. They change the game for everyone, and they do not reverse.
