## The setup

A middle-market company has assets worth $V$ and owes a single zero-coupon debt of face $D$ due at $T$. At maturity, shareholders pay the debt if $V_T > D$ and keep $V_T - D$; otherwise they walk away and the lenders take the assets. Shareholders therefore hold a call on $V$ with strike $D$:

$$
E = V\,N(d_1) - D e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad
d_1 = \frac{\ln(V/D) + (r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_A^2)\,T}{\sigma_A\sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_A\sqrt{T}
$$

The debt is worth $V - E$. Its yield is $y = -\ln(B/D)/T$, and the credit spread is $y - r$. The risk-neutral probability of default is $N(-d_2)$.

## Reading the outputs

**Spread versus leverage.** The three curves plot the implied spread against the ratio of the present value of debt to asset value, for asset volatilities of 15%, 25% and 40%. Two things stand out. First, the curves are convex: spread is nearly flat at low leverage and explodes past a threshold. Second, the volatility curves are far apart. At the same leverage, a borrower whose assets are twice as volatile can carry several times the spread. Private credit underwriting that focuses on leverage multiples and ignores asset volatility is reading the wrong axis.

**Distance to default.** The risk-neutral $d_2$ discounts at the risk-free rate because it is built for pricing. For forecasting defaults you want the real-world version, which replaces $r$ with the expected asset return $\mu$:

$$
\text{DD} = \frac{\ln(V/D) + (\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_A^2)\,T}{\sigma_A\sqrt{T}}
$$

The model shows both. The real-world default probability is lower than the risk-neutral one whenever $\mu > r$, which is the normal case, and the gap is the credit risk premium.

**Equity delta.** $\partial E/\partial V = N(d_1)$. At low leverage delta is near one: shareholders own the assets in all but name. As leverage rises, delta falls, and shareholders become option holders. Option holders like volatility. That is why a distressed borrower's management will take the long-shot acquisition or the risky pivot: equity gains from the upside, debt absorbs the downside. Covenants, collateral, and lender step-in rights are the mechanism design that pushes back.

**Equity volatility.** The identity $\sigma_E = N(d_1)\,V\sigma_A / E$ links the unobservable asset volatility to the observable equity volatility. In practice, that is how $V$ and $\sigma_A$ are backed out from a listed company's stock. For a private borrower you do not have $\sigma_E$, which is one reason private credit marks are smoother than they should be.

## The game inside the model

Lender and borrower are playing a game the Merton model makes explicit. The borrower chooses $\sigma_A$ after the loan is made. The lender prices the loan on the $\sigma_A$ it expects. If the lender cannot constrain volatility, the borrower's best response once in distress is to raise it, and the lender's best response ex ante is to charge for that possibility. Covenant-lite structures remove the constraint and the pricing should reflect it. Where it does not, the mispricing sits with the lender, which in private credit is increasingly a retail-facing fund.

## What this means for investors

If you hold private credit through a fund, the questions to ask are the model's inputs: what is the asset volatility of the borrowers, not just their leverage; how far is the typical borrower from the point where the spread curve goes vertical; and what mechanisms stop a distressed borrower from doing what option holders rationally do. The spread you are paid is only generous relative to the right answers.
